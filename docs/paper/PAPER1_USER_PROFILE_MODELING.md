## 1. Abstract — rewrite

Your instinct is right ("interests **and** their various favorite actions") — that two-part
framing is exactly the contribution and no prior work does both. But the current abstract
states a problem and stops; it never states a result.

An abstract needs five moves: problem → gap → method → result → significance.

**Draft:**

> Online deliberation forums stall when participants cannot find the discussions they are
> actually equipped to contribute to. Recommender systems address this, but they can only be
> as good as their model of the user. Existing approaches rely on interaction-frequency
> signals such as collaborative filtering, which degrade under cold start and, more
> fundamentally, model *what* a user engages with while ignoring *how* they contribute.
> We present a user-profiling mechanism that models both dimensions: a participant's topical
> interests and their preferred deliberation moves (proposing a barrier, a solution, an
> analogy, a failure mode, and so on). The profile is a per-agent decision tree learned
> online from the participant's own clickstream, replacing a statically declared interest
> table, and it re-derives itself from the full interaction history rather than accumulating
> against a fixed hierarchy. Validating such a profile is normally impossible because a real
> user's true preferences are unobservable, so we introduce an evaluation harness of
> generative agents equipped with hidden, deterministic preference models: the profile is
> scored against an answer key it never sees. On a 70-tick deliberation simulation the
> learned profiles recover **90.6%** of their agents' true engage/skip boundaries, including
> over regions of the idea space the agent was never shown. The harness also exposes two
> effects that are invisible without ground truth: profile quality is upper-bounded by the
> behavioural *consistency* of the user, and a profile trained inside a recommendation loop
> is measurably biased by that loop's own selections.

Note what this does: it names a **specific number**, and it promises the two findings
(consistency ceiling, selection bias) that make the paper more than an engineering report.

## 2. Introduction

### 2.1 Background — expand your Zhang et al. paragraph

Your bullets are correct but compressed. Expand into three paragraphs.

**Paragraph 1 — the generative-agent precedent.** Zhang et al. ("On Generative Agents in
Recommendation") introduced Agent4Rec, a simulator in which LLM-driven generative agents
stand in for real users, each equipped with profile, memory, and action modules. Their
research question was whether such agents can *reproduce* the behaviour of real autonomous
humans inside a recommender. State clearly that this establishes the methodology you build
on: generative agents are a legitimate testbed for recommender research.

**Paragraph 2 — how you differ, and why the difference matters.** Two departures, and be
explicit that the second is the substantive one:
- *Domain.* Agent4Rec operates over movies: a fixed catalogue of items with no
  relationship to one another. A deliberation map is a **structured artifact the users
  themselves build** — posts are typed (goal, barrier, cause, solution, abstraction, analogy)
  and connected in a purpose tree. The item space is generated by the interactions being
  modelled, so it is non-stationary by construction.
- *Direction of inquiry.* Agent4Rec asks whether agents can imitate humans. **We invert it:**
  we use agents whose preferences we *specify* in order to obtain ground truth, and ask
  whether a profiling mechanism can *recover* those preferences from behaviour alone. Their
  agents are the object of study; ours are the measuring instrument.

**Paragraph 3 — why the incumbent approach is insufficient.** Collaborative filtering infers
preference from co-occurrence in interaction frequency. Three failures in this setting:
(i) **cold start** — a new participant has no history, and in deliberation the *item* is also
new, since every post was authored moments ago, so item-side history is unavailable too;
(ii) **no action model** — CF predicts *whether* a user engages, never *how*, yet in
deliberation the same post admits a dozen distinct contributions and choosing the wrong one
is itself a failed recommendation; (iii) **no interpretability** — a latent factor cannot be
shown to the user or to a facilitator, whereas a deliberation profile should be legible to
the person it describes.

### 2.2 AIdeator — tighten and make the connection load-bearing

Right now this section says AIdeator exists but not why it matters to *profiling*. Fix that.

> AIdeator, developed by Professor Klein, is a structured deliberation methodology in which
> practitioners from different fields decompose a societal problem into a purpose tree —
> stakeholders, goals, barriers, causes, solutions — and extend it through analogical moves
> that abstract a problem, find a structural parallel in a distant domain, and translate the
> borrowed solution back.

Then the load-bearing sentence:

> AIdeator matters to profiling because it supplies a **typed action vocabulary**. Because
> every contribution is one of a closed set of moves, a user profile can be defined over
> (topic × move) rather than topic alone. This is what makes "favourite actions" a
> measurable object rather than a metaphor: an agent that reliably proposes barriers but
> never analogies has a profile shape that a topic-only model cannot express.

### 2.3 Motivation — your two bullets, developed

Your bullets are unfinished ("Dynamic Refinement: With agents it is much easier to"). Also
"can capture context better" understates it. Reframe as three claims:

1. **Ground truth becomes available.** This is the real motivation and should be first. For a
   human user, the true preference model is unobservable — you can only ever measure proxies
   like click-through. With generative agents you *specify* the preference model, so profile
   inference becomes a supervised problem with an answer key. You can state, as a number, how
   well the profile understood the person.
2. **The profile can be refined continuously and re-derived from scratch.** Because every
   interaction is persisted, the profile is not an accumulator that drifts against a fixed
   hierarchy; each retrain rebuilds it from the complete history. A structural revision does
   not require reconciling legacy state.
3. **Behaviour is reproducible.** Real users are noisy and unrepeatable. Agents can be held
   fixed while the profiling mechanism is varied — a genuine ablation, impossible with humans.

## 3. Architecture / Methods

This is the core of Paper #1 and your draft's four bullets are far too thin. Structure it as
five subsections. **The single most important thing to make clear is that there are two
distinct models and readers will conflate them.**

### 3.1 The deliberation simulation (the testbed — keep brief)
3–8 generative agents with distinct expert personas (economist, engineer, environmentalist,
psychologist, policy-maker, innovator, sociologist, strategist) collaboratively grow a
deliberation map rooted at a mission. Each tick, every agent is shown one (post, action) pair
and either **engages** — authoring a typed child post — or **skips**. Actions are constrained
by post type: a goal admits a barrier, a solution, or an abstraction; a solution admits an
improvement, a part, a failure mode, a barrier, or a question. This is the environment; the
profiling mechanism is the contribution.

### 3.2 Two models, and why the distinction is the paper

Give this its own subsection with a figure. Readers *will* conflate these.

| | **Generative model** | **Inferential model** |
|---|---|---|
| what it is | the agent's hidden true preferences | the system's learned profile |
| role | **decides** engage/skip | **predicts** engage/skip |
| visibility | never seen by the profiler | built only from observed behaviour |
| analogy | a person's actual taste | a service's model of that taste, built from clicks |

The generative model gives each agent ~10 liked and ~10 disliked subtopics (LLM-generated
from persona + mission, embedded as centroids). The decision rule is deterministic geometry:

> engage ⟺ max cos(post, liked) ≥ max cos(post, disliked)

State explicitly that **no LLM is involved in the decision** — only in writing content once
the decision is "engage," so post embeddings remain realistic. Justify the choice: it buys
perfect behavioural consistency *and* the answer key, at the cost of naturalism (§6).

### 3.3 The profile: a decision tree over the clickstream

Describe the pipeline as a numbered method:

1. **Log.** Every recommendation outcome is persisted as (post embedding, engaged/skipped,
   tick). All events are kept permanently.
2. **Reduce.** Post embeddings (3072-d) are PCA-reduced to 50 dimensions.
3. **Fit.** A per-agent `DecisionTreeClassifier` (entropy criterion, `min_samples_leaf = 3`
   to prevent over-partitioning) maps reduced embedding → engage/skip.
4. **Refresh.** Retrained from the complete clickstream on a schedule — aggressively during
   the early phase, then less often once the model stabilises.
5. **Label.** Each leaf is named post-hoc by an LLM from the actual posts that fall inside it.

Then state the design commitments explicitly, because these are the arguments:
- **Behaviour over declaration.** The predecessor was a statically declared interest table —
  ten topics an LLM guessed from the persona text, frozen before any interaction. It was
  never validated against behaviour. A guess about a user is not a model of one.
- **Rebuild, don't accumulate.** Retraining from raw events means a revised hierarchy
  redistributes history automatically; there is no legacy table to reconcile.
- **Interpretable by construction of its leaves.** The split conditions are PCA components
  and are *not* individually meaningful — say so plainly rather than overclaiming. The
  interpretable layer is the leaf: a named cluster with a purity and a sample count.

### 3.4 Profiling actions, not just topics

This subsection delivers the "favourite actions" half of your abstract and is your clearest
novelty. Each profile header carries a predicted score over the **13 deliberation moves**
(goal, barrier, cause, solution, abstraction, analogy, inspiration, question, answer, pro,
con, part, failure mode), alongside the *observed* counts from the clickstream.

Give the worked example — it is concrete and persuasive:

> For TheEconomist under the header *Land Value Capture*, the profile predicts
> solution 0.90, pro 0.90, barrier 0.80, and rates analogy and inspiration low. The
> profile therefore encodes not merely that this participant is interested in land value
> capture, but that they will argue *for* mechanisms and name obstacles rather than reach for
> cross-domain analogies.

Make the general claim: a topic-only profile is a vector over subjects; this profile is a
matrix over (subject × move), and the recommendation that follows from it is a *pair*, not
an item.

### 3.5 Reports as profile evidence
Every skip triggers a follow-up in which the agent states, in its own voice, why the
recommendation was a poor fit. These are qualitative evidence for the profile and are what
produced the paper's central diagnostic finding (§4.1). Note that they must be elicited with
a prose system prompt — reusing the agent's JSON-forcing prompt returns the reason wrapped in
a JSON action object.

## 4. Experiments / Results

**Replace your current Results paragraph entirely.** It reports "50 ticks, skip rate decreased
12%, still 42%" — that is (a) the superseded LLM-agent plateau, and (b) a recommender metric.
For Paper #1 the headline is inference accuracy.

### 4.1 Result 1 — what a profile must actually model

Present the skip-reason analysis as a finding, since it justifies the architecture:
mined skip reasons divide roughly evenly between **topical mismatch** ("my expertise lies more
in behavioural psychology") and **redundancy** ("the existing explanation sufficiently covers
this"). Critically, **skip rate did not vary meaningfully by action type — every move sat in a
40–55% band.** A profile keyed on action type alone is therefore not predictive; the
discriminative signal is *which post*, in topical relation to the participant. Report this as
a negative result that redirected the design.

### 4.2 Result 2 — headline: profiles recover 90.6% of true preferences

Define the metric precisely, because its construction is the methodological contribution:

> The learned tree is asked to classify **every post in the session** and compared against the
> hidden ground-truth model's decision for that post. Because the tree trained only on the
> ~70 posts its agent was actually shown, but is evaluated over all ~194, this is a
> **generalisation test** over unseen regions of the idea space — not a fit statistic.

**Table 1 — Inference accuracy (70-tick run, 4 agents):**

| agent | vs ground truth | train accuracy | gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| TheEconomist | 96.9% | 100.0% | 3.1 |
| TheEngineer | 93.8% | 100.0% | 6.2 |
| TheInnovator | 89.1% | 97.2% | 8.1 |
| TheEnvironmentalist | 82.8% | 100.0% | **17.2** |
| **mean** | **90.6%** | | |

Report the 6-agent 50-tick run (**87.6%** mean) as a second condition. Be candid that two of
its agents scored a trivial 100% because their ground-truth models liked essentially
everything they were shown, collapsing the tree to a constant predictor — the informative
band is 82–97%.

### 4.3 Result 3 — the consistency ceiling

This is a genuine finding and deserves its own subsection.

> A profile can be no more accurate than the user is self-consistent. With agents deciding by
> free LLM judgment, the same post could receive different decisions on different ticks; under
> that condition profile-driven prediction plateaued and no amount of additional data improved
> it. Replacing free judgment with a deterministic preference model removed the noise floor
> and made the boundary learnable.

State the implication for practice: before attributing poor recommendation quality to the
recommender, one should measure the *user's* behavioural consistency, which upper-bounds
achievable accuracy. For human deployments this suggests reporting an estimated consistency
alongside any accuracy claim.

### 4.4 Result 4 — profiles are biased by the loop that trains them

The `train_accuracy` column is not padding; it carries the paper's most transferable finding.

> TheEnvironmentalist's profile scored **100% on the posts it was trained on but 82.8%
> against ground truth** — a 17-point gap. In the 6-agent condition the same agent showed
> 98% versus 60%, a 38-point gap. The profile is trained on a **non-random sample the system
> itself selected**: as the recommender improves, it increasingly shows only predicted-likes,
> so the profile sees a narrowing slice of the preference space and overfits it.

Emphasise that this is intrinsic to any closed-loop personalisation system and is normally
*invisible* — without ground truth you would observe only the reassuring 100% training figure.
Recommend that closed-loop profilers report a train/truth gap as a standard diagnostic. Note
the mitigation direction — exploration widens the sample — and that in our runs the condition
with more forced exploration showed the smaller gap.

### 4.5 Result 5 — interpretability

Show a real learned profile rather than asserting legibility:

> TheEngineer's tree separated *"Transit Accessibility & AI"* (0% engage, entropy 0.00,
> n = 21) from *"Decentralized Energy Hubs"* (100% engage, entropy 0.00, n = 20), with a
> small mixed leaf *"Advanced AI & Energy"* (33% engage, entropy 0.92, n = 3) that the purity
> measure correctly flags as low-confidence.

Both large leaves are perfectly pure, semantically coherent, and were discovered from
behaviour alone — the names were assigned afterward from member posts. The entropy column
lets a consumer of the profile distinguish confident regions from uncertain ones.

## 5. Discussion

Do not restate results. Argue three positions:

**5.1 A user profile should be a learned object with an error bar.** The field routinely ships
profiles that are never validated because validation appears impossible. It is not impossible
— it requires constructing a population whose preferences you control. Once you do, "how well
does the system understand this user" becomes a reportable number, and the profile stops being
an implementation detail and becomes an evaluable artifact.

**5.2 Profiling actions is a distinct problem from profiling interests.** Recommending the
right topic with the wrong move is still a failed recommendation. Our negative result (§4.1)
shows action type is not predictable from action statistics; it is predictable from
(topic × persona). Any domain with a typed interaction vocabulary — code review, structured
Q&A, annotation, tutoring — inherits this.

**5.3 Closed-loop profiles require honest accounting.** §4.4 generalises well beyond
deliberation. Any system that trains a user model on data its own recommender selected is
partly grading its own homework. The train/truth gap is a cheap, general diagnostic and we
argue it should be standard.

## 6. Limitations — write this section, do not omit it

Reviewers will find these; naming them first is strength.

- **Synthetic agents are consistent but not human.** The geometric decision rule is what makes
  ground truth possible and is also what makes the agents unlike people. Results establish
  that the mechanism *can* recover a preference structure; they do not establish accuracy
  against human users. The next step is estimating human consistency and re-validating.
- **Single-run evidence.** Runs with nominally similar settings differed substantially,
  so individual runs cannot resolve small effects. Reported accuracies should be treated as
  point estimates; conditions requiring replication are identified as such.
- **The benchmark has a difficulty knob.** The margin in the ground-truth decision rule
  controls task difficulty, and outcomes are highly sensitive to it via a feedback loop — a
  stricter rule yields fewer engagements, hence fewer posts, hence a thinner candidate pool.
  Comparisons are only valid with the margin held fixed. Report its value.
- **Accuracy degrades as a metric under class imbalance.** When one class falls below roughly
  8% of decisions, the tree has too few counterexamples and accuracy becomes uninformative
  even as it stays high. Report class balance beside every accuracy figure.
- **Split conditions are not interpretable.** Legibility comes from post-hoc leaf labelling,
  not from the tree's internal structure. Embeddings are not invertible without a trained
  decoder.

## 7. Future Work

Keep your two bullets, both are good, and add three that follow from the findings:

- **Scale.** Hundreds of agents over thousands of ticks, to test whether profile quality
  saturates and whether profiles specialise or converge in a shared population.
- **Deployment in AIdeator with human participants**, including the "user recovery" case where
  a profile is reconstructed for a returning participant from partial history.
- **Human consistency estimation.** Measure how self-consistent real deliberators are; this
  sets the achievable accuracy ceiling for any deployed profile (§4.3).
- **Exploration policies for debiasing.** §4.4 gives a measurable objective: minimise the
  train/truth gap while holding engagement acceptable — a bandit-style trade-off with an
  unusually direct evaluation signal.
- **Richer feature sets.** The profile currently learns from post embeddings alone. Action
  type, tree position, post age, and skip-reason text are all available and unexploited;
  classifier feature importances would show which earn their cost.
